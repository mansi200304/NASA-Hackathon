How we measured

  1. Radar measures distance. A satellite sends a radar pulse down and times the echo, so it knows how far away the ground is.
  2. Compare two passes. The same satellite flies over the same spot every 6 to 12 days. If the ground moved between two passes, the radar waves return slightly out of step. That phase change is the movement, to a few millimetres.
  3. Add up many pairs. One pair is noisy, because air moisture and the ionosphere also shift the signal. Chaining many pairs over years lets the steady sinking add up while the random noise does not.
  4. Two satellites. Sentinel-1 (C-band) has flown since 2016, so it gives a ten-year trend. NASA-ISRO NISAR (L-band) started in late 2025, so its record is still short. We show NISAR on top and Sentinel-1 underneath so you can see the trend behind it.
  5. Two look directions. Satellites look at the ground from the east or the west. When both agree, the sinking is much more likely to be real.

Corrections we applied

Solid-earth tide and ionosphere were removed from every Sentinel-1 and NISAR pair. Water vapour (troposphere) was not removed, which is a main source of the remaining noise.

Limits

  • NISAR is new. It has under a year of data, with gaps, and its newest data is Provisional. Its rates here are not reliable yet; we show them with their uncertainty and say so.
  • Line of sight, not straight down. Radar measures motion toward or away from the satellite. We assume subsidence is mostly vertical, but our numbers are not pure vertical values.
  • Reference choice. Each satellite frame is measured against its own median ground. This can shift rates by a few millimetres per year.
  • Sinking does not prove who caused it. Farmland nearby fits groundwater pumping for irrigation, but it is not proof.
  • Mines look like sinking too. Open-pit mines and tailings move for other reasons. We mark the ones we found.
  • Radar loses signal over plowed fields and changing ground. Gaps in the map are places with no reliable measurement, not places with no sinking.
  • Blue patches are probably not real uplift. Rising values, such as on the Willcox Playa floor, most likely come from wet or swelling soil changing the radar signal. We have not verified this.
  • Radar cannot see wildlife. Habitat links come from outside maps.
  • Compared with ADWR, with limits. Over the same pixels our rates match ADWR's own InSAR products closely in the southeast and weakly at Eloy. ADWR's Sentinel-1 product is the same satellite as ours, so that match is a consistency check, not independent proof; RADARSAT-2 is independent.
