OUR STORY / WHY WE BUILT SINKRONIZED

Arizona is our home.What happens beneath it matters.

For us, Arizona is more than a place on a map. It is where we live, learn and imagine our future. Its desert landscapes, expanding cities and agricultural communities are connected by something most of us rarely see: groundwater.

We began with a question that felt close to home. What happens when the land itself starts to change, slowly enough that most people never notice? In Arizona’s alluvial basins, decades of groundwater extraction have lowered water levels and compacted sediment, contributing to subsidence and earth fissures. This is documented in USGS research. These changes can put roads, canals, wells and buildings at risk.

The evidence also identifies significant water-use pressures. In the Willcox Basin, agriculture accounts for most estimated water use, while a 2023 peer-reviewed study found agricultural expansion strongly associated with groundwater declines. Those findings are important, but the research is often buried in technical datasets and reports. People deserve a clearer way to understand what is happening—and what remains uncertain.

WHY WE CREATED THIS

We built Sinkronized to turn satellite observations into an understandable picture of Arizona’s changing ground. By combining radar measurements from Sentinel-1 and NISAR with comparisons to Arizona water-agency research, we can explore where movement is observed, how it changes over time and how much confidence we should place in the evidence.

We should name the causes where the evidence supports them: groundwater overdraft and irrigation pumping have contributed to land subsidence in particular Arizona basins. But this differs from assigning a particular coloured pixel to one farm, well or development. Our aim is to make that distinction transparent, so people can ask better questions about the ground and water we share.

WHAT WE ARE STUDYING

Not just whether the land moves. How confidently we can say so.

We investigate ground movement in the study areas supplied by our team’s current dataset. Satellite radar reveals changes along its viewing direction; real acquisition dates, stable reference areas, data quality and comparisons with Arizona Department of Water Resources research help us interpret those signals.

We distinguish the longer Sentinel-1 record from newer, preliminary NISAR measurements. Where the evidence is weak or missing, the website says so instead of presenting a guess as a result.

How do two satellite viewing directions help? +

Satellites make ascending (generally south-to-north) and descending (generally north-to-south) passes, observing the land from different slanted angles. Comparing these perspectives can strengthen confidence and help separate some components of motion, but one view alone cannot establish a unique vertical sinking rate.
